Chapter II · game plan

VYUHA

A hidden-rank war of formations on a hexagonal field that turns. Twelve pieces, ten hidden ranks, one fort — and a board with no corners to hide in.

Working plan · supersedes the square-arena chapter · 28 Oct 2026

01 · the idea

One sentence

You are a vyuha-acharya — a formation-master — and the battlefield is a formation, not a grid. You place a shape, the enemy places a shape, and the shape itself becomes the fight.

Hidden, but readable

Ranks stay face-down until they fight. What you can read is behaviour: what has moved, what refuses to move, what the odds say. Every guess is a calculation, never a coin flip.

The field moves

Every fifth round the inner rings turn one cell and expose whatever they carry. There is no safe square to camp on, so the game cannot stall.

Direction matters

Every piece faces a hex edge. A strike from behind ignores rank. Turning your back is a real decision, and so is spending a turn to face the right way.

02 · the board

Padmavyuha — the lotus field

A hexagon of hexagons: an eye at the centre, three rings around it, 37 cells. Six-fold symmetry means every approach is equal, and no corner is a fortress.

Outer ring — the field (18)
Second ring — the turn (12)
Inner ring — the turn (6)
The eye — hub, one cell (1)

Tap a cell to see its neighbours. Six exits everywhere except the hub, which touches all six inner cells.

37 cells. Twelve pieces a side, deployed in three petals each — 15 cells per player, so three spare cells to arrange with.

Cells37 (1 + 6 + 12 + 18)Dense enough for contact by round 6, open enough to manoeuvre

03 · the rules

Six rules, all of them ours

RULE 01

Hidden ranks

Ten classes, ranks 1–10, plus a trap and a fort. A clash is decided by rank; a piece is only revealed when it fights or rides the turn.

RULE 02

Facing

Every piece faces one of six edges, set by its last move. Strike a piece from the rear — the opposite edge — and its rank does not count. Champions must guard their backs.

RULE 03

The turn

On rounds 5, 10, 15… the inner two rings rotate one cell. Anything carried is revealed. Fast lanes, public exposure.

RULE 04

Powers

Three classes act without moving: the seer scans two cells and reveals, the archer volleys exactly two cells along a lane, the cavalry dashes two cells and strikes.

RULE 05

Fort and trap

Capture the fort and the match ends. The trap kills anything that strikes it except the engineer, which dismantles it. Both are immovable — and that is a tell.

RULE 06

The clock

Sixty orders without a capture is a draw. Scenarios run on rounds instead. No game can stall, and the countdown is always visible.

04 · why this is not Stratego

A different game that shares a feeling

Hidden ranks on a battlefield is a genre, not a property — but a genre can be copied too closely. The design deliberately diverges on the things that define that game.

StrategoVyuha
FieldSquare grid, 10×10Hexagonal field, 37 cells, six exits
SetupTwo mirrored halves, 40 piecesThree petals each, 12 pieces, asymmetric shapes
BoardStaticInner rings rotate every fifth round
DirectionIrrelevantFacing decides whether rank counts
PowersNoneScan, volley, dash
InformationGuessworkOdds, intel ledger, behavioural tells
EndingFlag or attritionFort capture, elimination, or the round clock
WorldNapoleonicEpic-age Indian formations and dynasties
Not legal advice — I am not a lawyer. The design is built for mechanical distance rather than legal certainty: geometry, movement, information, board behaviour and win conditions all differ. If this is going commercial, have an IP lawyer read the ruleset before launch.
05 · the myth

The Vyuhacharya

The epics do not remember battles by their kings but by their shapes — the wheel that closed on a boy, the lotus that opened, the needle that pierced. This is a game about the people who drew those shapes: the formation-masters, the acharyas, whose knowledge was carried, lost and rebuilt.

You are the last student of a broken tradition. Seven formations survive as fragments — a diagram on a palm leaf, a verse, a scar. Rebuild each one, and you learn what it cost the first time it was used.

The wheel. Concentric rings, a turning centre, a fort in the eye. The lesson: a formation that closes can also trap its maker.

II
Padmavyuha पद्मव्यूहchapter II

The lotus. Six petals opening on a hex field. The lesson: a shape that opens invites a strike from behind.

III
Kraunchavyuha क्रौञ्चव्यूह

The crane. A long neck and a wide body: a narrow corridor into a broad field. The lesson: choke points are won before the first move.

IV
Makaravyuha मकरव्यूह

The crocodile. Two jaws that close. The lesson: pincer, never push.

V
Suchivyuha सूचीव्यूह

The needle. A single lane, a single purpose. The lesson: an army that can only go one way must go fast.

VI
Shyenavyuha श्येनव्यूह

The hawk. A diving attack from an open sky. The lesson: reach beats strength.

VII
Sarvatobhadra सर्वतोभद्र

Auspicious on every side — the perfect square that defends all approaches and therefore threatens none. The lesson: the last formation is the one you invent.

A deliberate choice: the game uses formations, dynasties and army classes — never deities as pieces. Nothing holy is ever captured. The mythology lives in the shapes, the names, the art direction and the story of the acharyas, which keeps it respectful and still unmistakably Indian.
06 · the shell

Layers, tabs, and a way back

One rule for the whole interface: every screen you can go deeper into, you can back out of — with the same gesture, in the same place, every time.

1 · Thresholdcommander profiles, sign-in→
2 · Hall of Vyuhascampaign map, seven chapters→
3 · Chapterthe formation, its lesson, its reward→
4 · War councilscenario, board, difficulty, clock→
5 · Legendtwo lines of story, then deploy→
6 · Deploymentplace, swap, lock→
7 · Battlethe board, and nothing else→
8 · Reckoningresult, lesson learned, next chapter

Information lives in tabs

The battle screen stays clean: board, strips, plate. Everything else is a tab that slides in from the side and slides back out.

ArmyIntelLogCodexSettings
  • Army — your twelve, what is revealed, what is spent
  • Intel — enemy unknowns, odds, behavioural tells
  • Log — every order, in plain language
  • Codex — classes, powers, edge cases

Big, clean, one idea per screen

Pieces get bigger because the board owns the screen; labels get bigger because they sit on plates, not in text. Nothing competes with the field.

08 · the plan

Four phases, each one shippable

Phase 1
field

The hex field plays

  • New geometry: 37 cells, petals, rotation rings, rays for six directions
  • Facing: stored on every piece, set by its move, checked in combat
  • Deployment in three petals, forts on the outer ring
  • AI updated; rules and self-play tests extended to the hex board
Phase 2
shell

The layered interface

  • Chapter map, chapter page, war council, legend, battle, reckoning
  • Tabs for Army / Intel / Log / Codex with a consistent back
  • Bigger tokens, bigger plates, less chrome
Phase 3
story

Seven chapters

  • One scenario per vyuha, each teaching one mechanic
  • Progress saved per profile; a lesson written on completion
  • Campaign map art, chapter medallions
Phase 4
polish

Feel

  • Piece portraits, chapter art, tension cue for the final rounds
  • Animation pass: rotation, dash trails, capture slide
  • Accessibility, colour-blind checks, reduced motion
09 · decisions

Three things to settle before Phase 1

Facing — in or out?

It is the most distinctive rule we have and the most complex to learn. My recommendation: in, but introduced in chapter III, not on the first board.

Keep the wheel?

Chakravyuha already exists and plays well. Recommendation: keep it as chapter I and add the hex as chapter II, rather than replacing it.

Portraits?

Ten character portraits would make the pieces read like people. Recommendation: after Phase 1, so the field is right before the faces arrive.
