Design studio · Aetherfront chapter II

VYUHAव्यूह — the formation

A battlefield is a shape before it is a game. The epics knew this: an army was a vyūha — a lotus, a needle, a wheel — and the whole battle was about which shape answered which. Three new boards, each with a rule only that shape can carry.

Tap any tile on the boards below to see how movement changes when the square is gone.

01

Why geometry, and why Indian

A reskin changes how a game looks. A new lattice changes how it thinks. Every board here earns its place by adding exactly one rule that is impossible on a square grid — and each one is drawn from a formation that already had a name in Indian military literature.

Lineage

Chaturanga & ashtapada

Four limbs of an army — elephants, chariots, cavalry, infantry — on an eight-by-eight grid. The square board is not foreign to India; it is the ancestor. Keep it, and call it Sarvatobhadra: "auspicious on every side".

Vocabulary

Vyūha, not "map"

Chakravyuha (wheel), Padmavyuha (lotus), Suchivyuha (needle), Kraunchavyuha (crane). These are battle formations from the epics — tactical, not devotional. Naming a board after a formation is naming it after a plan, which is what a board is.

Respect

Classes, not deities

Pieces are army classes — रथ chariot, गज elephant, चर spy, दुर्ग fort. No god is a playing piece, no deity is "captured". The mythology enters through formations and motifs, which is where it belongs.

02

Three battlefields

Each board is playable at 12 pieces a side. Cell counts are deliberately close to the current 48, so match length stays in the 6–12 minute band.

Tap a tile: gold = selected, saffron = legal step. On the wheel, the centre is one cell.

04

Two art directions

Both keep the hidden-rank clarity: a piece must read at 24 px on a phone. Both replace emoji with etched vector marks.

Direction A · recommended

Temple-punk — brass & obsidian

Carved stone and inlaid brass, lit like a lamp-lit mandapa. Boards are drawn as yantra linework: concentric rings, petal arcs, kolam dot-grids. Pieces are engraved discs with a single glyph and a rank notch cut into the rim — the physicality of an old chaturanga set, rendered in vectors.

Rifts become māyā — a cut of starfield through the stone. Selection glows like a lamp wick.

Direction B · alternate

Astral yantra — nebula & filigree

Keep the current sci-fi glass, but hang gold filigree over it: the mandala becomes an orbital diagram, the lotus becomes a star-chart, the wheel becomes a gyroscope. Bridges this chapter to the existing Aetherfront look without throwing away the art you already have.

Best if you want one universe across chapters rather than a new skin per board.

05

Five new ways to play

Geometry gives you boards; these give you reasons to come back. Ordered by how cheap they are to build on the existing engine.

  • 01
    Abhimanyu — the breach. Asymmetric scenario: you have six pieces and twenty turns to break into a rotating chakravyuha and take the fort; the defender has twelve. A story every Indian child knows, turned into a countdown. Cheapest to build: it is the existing game with a turn limit and a piece handicap.
  • 02
    Akshauhini draft. Twelve slots, a point budget, ten classes with costs — both sides build different armies before deploying. The Suryavansh can field two Paladins and no Lancer; the Chandravansh can buy a third Mine. Turns a fixed puzzle into a meta.
  • 03
    Vyuha cards. Pick one formation card before the match: Padma (+1 rank when you pincer), Krauncha (scouts +1 range), Sarpa (a second mine), Shyena (first strike reveals instead of kills). Small, draftable, replayable.
  • 04
    Rana — the duel. No fort. You win by capturing the enemy सेनापति (Senapati). The Core becomes an ordinary heavy piece, so the endgame is a hunt, not a raid.
  • 05
    Andhakar — blind vyuhas. After you lock your formation, the war council shuffles it. You deploy in the dark and must discover your own army as it fights. Hidden information cuts both ways.
06

How it ships in your repo

The current engine is square-locked in three places. Fix those and every board above is data, not code.

  • 01
    Cells, not coordinates. Replace board[r][c] with a cell list: cells: [{id, x, y, shape, neighbours:[id…]}] and board: {cellId: unit}. The engine already talks in {r,c}; make it talk in cellId and nothing else changes.
  • 02
    Neighbour lists, not directions. computeValidTargets loops over four fixed directions. Loop over cell.neighbours instead — six on the lotus, three on the needle, eight-ish on the wheel. Range becomes "steps along a path of neighbours", which the Strider already needs.
  • 03
    Render from geometry. Each cell carries its own SVG path or polygon points, so the board renderer becomes a loop over cells. The existing tile classes (selected, target, rift, last-move) all survive untouched.
  • 04
    Then the three rules. Pincer (lotus) is a lookup of shared neighbours; rotation (wheel) is a permutation of the cell list every five turns; facing (needle) is one extra field on the unit. All three are ~40 lines each.

Effort estimate: the graph refactor is the only real work — roughly a day of careful change plus re-running the 40 rule tests. After that, each new board is an afternoon, because the engine no longer knows what a square is.
